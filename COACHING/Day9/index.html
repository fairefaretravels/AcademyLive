Challenge 9

Meeting Recovery

⚡

Hook

(High energy. Smile in your voice before you even say a word.)

Here's a scenario I bet you know too well. You leave a meeting. Somebody says "great talk, let's follow up on that." Two days later, you genuinely cannot remember what "that" was.

It's not your memory failing you. It's that most meetings produce a pile of raw notes and zero actual structure. Today we fix that — same skill from Day One, pointed at your messiest meeting notes instead of a blank chat box.

Let's recover a real meeting, live.

📖

Story

Meet Aisha. Aisha runs weekly check-ins with her small team, and she takes notes the way most of us do — fast, messy, half in shorthand, scattered across whatever app was open at the time. After the meeting, those notes just... sat there. Decisions got made and then quietly forgotten. Action items lived only in people's heads, until they didn't.

She tried pasting her notes into AI and asking it to "summarize this." She got back a summary, sure — but it was just a shorter, still-vague version of the mess. It didn't know what actually mattered in that meeting versus what was just chatter.

The fix was simple once she found it: Aisha started telling AI exactly what to pull out — decisions, who owns what, and by when. Not "summarize" — "extract these three specific things."

Suddenly the same raw notes turned into a clean list her team could actually act on. Nothing got lost. Nobody had to remember anything from memory alone.

🎯

Lesson

Same rule, applied to the meeting room:

Vague in, vague out. Specific in, useful out.

For meetings, "specific" means naming three things:

  1. Decisions. What was actually agreed on, not just discussed?

  2. Owners. Who is responsible for each next step — by name, not "the team"?

  3. Deadlines. By when? Even a rough one is better than none.

Decisions, owners, deadlines. Ask for exactly those three things, and a wall of raw notes turns into something your team can actually run on.

🧪

Demo

Let's recover a real meeting live.

Step one — the vague version.

"Summarize my meeting notes."

(Pause.)

You get a summary — but it's soft. It restates what was said without telling you what actually needs to happen next.

Step two — let's fix it.

"Here are my raw notes from today's meeting: [paste them]. Pull out every decision that was made, who owns each next step, and any deadline mentioned — even a rough one. Format it as a short action list, not a narrative summary."

(Pause.)

Now look. A real action list. Names attached. Dates attached. Something you could send to the whole team in the next two minutes.

Step three — building your standing meeting-recovery prompt.

Here's the reusable version:

"From now on, when I paste in raw meeting notes, extract every decision made, the owner of each next step, and any deadline mentioned. If an owner or deadline isn't clear, flag it as 'needs owner' or 'needs deadline' instead of guessing. Format it as a short action list."

Save it. Run your real notes from your last meeting through it before tomorrow.

If you're watching live, drop your messiest meeting notes in chat — I'll clean a piece of them live.

🏆

Challenge

Your one thing today: recover one real meeting — pull up actual notes or a transcript and turn it into a decisions-owners-deadlines action list. Fifteen minutes. That's the win.

💭

Reflection

What surprised you about what counted as a "decision" once you actually looked for it?

What worked — did the action list catch something you'd already half-forgotten?

What would you do differently — is there a meeting habit, like naming an owner out loud, you'll start doing now?

If your honest answer is "half our meetings don't actually end with clear owners" — that's the lesson landing, and now you know what to fix live in the room, not just after.

👋

Closing

Don't chase tools, learn the skill. Note-taking apps and transcription tools will keep changing. Knowing to ask for decisions, owners, and deadlines — that works no matter what's recording the meeting.

Your memory isn't the problem. An unstructured pile of notes is the problem. You just fixed that.
